> One day we'll be considered artists with our bespoke manual code.

A friend sent me that, most likely tongue-in-cheek. It stuck with me anyway.

You've probably seen the discourse about how [coding is solved](https://x.com/mattpocockuk/status/2091434155561107647) (there's a great discussion between Matt Pocock and Boris Cherny in that thread) and how Software Engineers will be living on the streets in six months. The second half of that is outside the boundaries of reality for the most part, and it's a different post.

This one is part rant, part ode to what I think is becoming a lost art: coding with your hands and your brain.

Most of the coding is being offloaded to agents already. I don't think that reverses — we've passed the point of no return. AI writes code faster than any person can, and too many companies care about maximizing output. (Maximizing output and maximizing profit aren't the same thing, which is a rant for another day.)

So the question isn't whether agents write the code. They do. It's what happens to *you* while they do it.

**The thing that makes you good at using AI is the same thing you stop practicing when you only use AI.**

An agent is only as good as the direction you give it and the review you put it through. If you can hold the system in your head — how the pieces fit, where the state lives, what happens when this call fails — you get better output back. You catch the answer that looks plausible and isn't. You know which of the three approaches it offered is the one you'll still be happy with later.

And when it breaks, you're still the one debugging it. That part didn't get automated. It got moved: from code you wrote and understood, to code you skimmed and approved.

## I'm not the only one landing here

Dreams of Code has a video called [Writing code by hand is now an advantage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bzYziksDslU), and the story at the center of it says what I keep failing to say.

He'd built the media engine for his video editor entirely with agents. It worked. It just wasn't *correct* — it kept breaking on the real, messy range of media files his users threw at it. And then comes the part that actually got me: he couldn't fix it, because he couldn't **evaluate** the fix. He had no way to tell whether the agent's next attempt was right or merely running. So he stopped, went and learned FFmpeg by building a media pipeline by hand, and rebuilt the thing.

That's the failure mode, and it isn't "the AI wrote bad code." The AI wrote code that ran. What he'd traded away was the ability to judge it.

He has a sharper example elsewhere in the same video: an agent reached for a mutex on a real-time audio thread. Perfectly reasonable-looking code. Also wrong, because blocking that thread drops samples, and the fix is a ring buffer. He caught it only because he'd once implemented that by hand and knew the constraint existed.

You cannot review your way to that. You either know it or you don't.

## That's the practical case. It isn't the real reason.

The real reason is that I liked it.

I've *always* enjoyed diving into the code — typing out the solution, figuring out the optimal implementation, weighing whether the thing I just wrote is actually readable. It's tons of little puzzles that, together, manifest a result that didn't exist in the world before. It was yours. It was the culmination of everything you'd learned up to that point, and the more you did it, the better you got (to a point).

What I miss is the small stuff. Reaching for the clever bit of syntax I picked up last month. Applying the pattern I finally understood. The hit of getting something *right*.

## Where he and I part ways

He gets to the same place I do, in the end — his last reason for writing code by hand, and the one he calls the most important, is simply that he enjoys it. Wasting time on something you enjoy, he figures, isn't wasted time. He calls what he's doing re-embracing it.

I can't quite get there. I believe every word of the practical case, and I'd make it in a room full of people who disagreed with me. But it isn't why I'm doing this, and I don't want to dress it up as a strategy.

The practical case has an expiration date. Maybe the models get good enough that holding the system in your head stops paying for itself. I doubt it, but maybe.

The other reason doesn't expire. I'm not going to stop, and I'm not going to pretend it's a productivity decision.
